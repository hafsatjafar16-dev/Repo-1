# UK Advertising Claims — ASA & the CAP Code

Working reference for cosmetic claims in UK-facing marketing (website, social,
packaging claims aimed at UK consumers, influencer/affiliate content). This
sits alongside, not instead of, the EU claims framework (Reg. 655/2013's six
common criteria — see `eu-1223-2009-overview.md` §8). A UK claim generally
needs to clear **both**: the UK Cosmetics Regulation's own claims rules
(near-identical to the EU six criteria, retained post-Brexit) **and** the
CAP Code's general advertising-standards bar.

Always verify current wording against the live CAP Code (code.asa.org.uk)
before it goes into a client deliverable — CAP guidance is updated more
often than people expect, especially around efficacy/testing evidence
standards.

## 1. Who's who

- **ASA (Advertising Standards Authority)** — the UK's independent ad
  regulator. Complaints-driven plus proactive monitoring. Enforces the
  **CAP Code** (non-broadcast: web, social, print, out-of-home) and the
  **BCAP Code** (broadcast) — cosmetics/beauty work is almost always a CAP
  Code question, not BCAP.
- **CAP (Committee of Advertising Practice)** — writes the Code and issues
  sector guidance; also runs a **free Copy Advice service** pre-clearance
  for non-broadcast ads. Worth recommending to clients for a genuinely
  higher-risk claim (novel efficacy claim, comparative claim, "clinically
  proven") before they spend on a campaign — it's non-binding but strongly
  indicative of how a complaint would be assessed.
- **Enforcement**: ASA rulings require an ad to be withdrawn/amended; they
  don't fine directly, but repeat or serious non-compliance gets referred to
  Trading Standards / the CMA, and rulings are public (reputational cost for
  a client is often the bigger deterrent than the ASA process itself).

## 2. What actually matters for cosmetic claims

- **Section 3 (Misleading advertising)** — the general substantiation bar:
  evidence for a claim must exist **before** the claim is made (not
  retrofitted after a complaint), must be robust enough for the strength of
  claim made, and must be held on file, not just referenced.
- **Section 12 (Medicines, medical devices, health-related products and
  beauty products)** — the section that actually governs most cosmetic
  efficacy claims: anti-ageing, "clinically proven," dermatologically
  tested, hypoallergenic, and similar. The general pattern: the stronger or
  more specific the claim, the more specific and robust the evidence has to
  be (a vague "look younger" claim needs less than a quantified "reduces
  wrinkles by X% in Y weeks" claim).
- **The medicinal-claim trap** — this is the same borderline-product issue
  as the EU side (see the overview doc's Art. 2 qualification note), just
  enforced by a different body: a claim that implies treating/curing/
  preventing a medical condition (acne, eczema, "heals," "treats") risks
  both an ASA ruling *and* reclassifying the product out of cosmetics
  entirely (MHRA borderline products territory) — flag this as a hard stop
  in claims review, not just a wording tweak.
- **"Free-from" and comparative claims** — same general EU sensitivity
  applies in the UK; CAP has specific guidance on both that's worth
  checking per campaign rather than assuming last year's answer still
  holds.

## 3. Practical workflow for client claims review

1. Classify each claim by risk tier: descriptive/sensory (low), performance/
   efficacy (medium-high), comparative or medical-adjacent (high).
2. For medium-high tier, confirm the client actually holds the substantiation
   (test data, consumer perception study, literature) *before* sign-off —
   don't take "we're confident this is true" as sufficient.
3. For genuinely novel or aggressive claims, recommend CAP Copy Advice before
   launch — cheap insurance against a costly reshoot/relaunch.
4. Keep the claims substantiation file alongside the PIF, even though it's
   not a strict PIF requirement in the same way as the CPSR — it's the first
   thing an ASA complaint or Trading Standards enquiry will ask for.

## 4. CTPA as a standing resource

The **CTPA (Cosmetic, Toiletry and Perfumery Association)** — the UK
cosmetics industry trade body — isn't a regulator itself, but its guidance,
Annex-change alerts, and position papers are a genuinely useful running
reference for both the UK regulatory picture and how the industry is
interpreting ambiguous claims/ingredient questions in practice. Worth
treating as a standing secondary source alongside EUR-Lex and the CAP Code,
particularly for UK-specific interpretation questions that the consolidated
legal text doesn't spell out directly.
